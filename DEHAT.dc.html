Project and programme

The money arrives as projects. The work accumulates as a programme.

The project

A grant arrives with a theme attached. Bridge classes, this time. For three years there is a centre in the village, a teacher on the register, children who had left school sitting in rows again. Then the funding period closes, the report is filed, the centre is dismantled. The report is accurate. It counts enrolments, attendance, transitions back into government schools. It has no line for the fourteen-year-old who worked out, in that room, that she was allowed to say no.

The programme

She stays. So does the village. Four years on, a different grant arrives with a different theme, and she is the one who convenes the meeting. The Child Parliament she chairs was nobody's deliverable. Nor was her mother, who can now name the entitlement she is owed and the office that owes it. Thirty-five of our forty-seven projects have landed in the same district, one after another, for twenty-five years. What has accumulated there appears in none of their reports.

Every closure report we have filed is true. None of them holds the part that lasted. So we record each intervention on three dimensions instead of one — who the community becomes, what it can now claim, and what visibly changed. Select an intervention to read all three.

Bahraich district, Uttar Pradesh · the Indo–Nepal border

In 1973 a family dispute in Champaran put a boy in a cowshed.

He was not the only one, and that was the part he could not leave alone. The boy was Jitendra Chaturvedi. Sixteen years later, in Bahraich, a group of young people opened a school for Tharu tribal and forest-dwelling children. The two threads did not meet for another eleven years.

Bahraich sits on the Nepal border. The Planning Commission once counted it among the hundred most backward districts in India; NITI Aayog now calls it an Aspirational District. DEHAT works in four such districts, and everything below happened here.

The Case For Investing

This Isn’t Charity. It’s an Investment in a Society We All Share.

Every society eventually pays the price of exclusion.

Whether through unemployment, poor health, food insecurity, environmental degradation, violence, migration, or lost economic potential, the costs of leaving people behind are never confined to those who experience them first. They ripple outward—affecting markets, institutions, businesses, governments, and communities alike.

At DEHAT, we therefore see social investment differently.

We do not believe that meaningful social change is driven by pity, charity, or the desire to “save” someone. Nor do we believe that communities are empty vessels waiting to be filled with external solutions.

People are not problems to be solved.

They are citizens, workers, farmers, entrepreneurs, parents, consumers, creators, and leaders whose potential is often constrained not by lack of ability, but by unequal access to opportunities, public systems, and justice.

Our work is about removing those barriers.

{{ cr.t }}

Every restored opportunity strengthens the social and economic fabric that all of us depend upon.

This is not a one-way transfer of generosity.

It is the strengthening of a shared ecosystem.

An economy is healthiest when more people can participate in it with dignity.

A village where families earn stable incomes buys more goods, demands better services, invests in education, improves housing, supports local enterprises, and creates opportunities for others. Businesses grow. Markets deepen. Public institutions become more accountable. Communities become more resilient.

Prosperity circulates.

That is the economy we are helping to build.

This is why we rarely describe our supporters as donors.

They are partners in building stronger communities, stronger institutions, and a stronger future for everyone.

{{ cs.not }} {{ cs.but }}

For over two decades, DEHAT has worked alongside communities—not above them—to ensure that children are protected, women exercise greater autonomy, farmers build resilient livelihoods, public systems become more accountable, and communities themselves become the drivers of lasting change.

Our role is not to stand in front of communities.

Our role is to stand beside them until they no longer need us.

Because real development is measured not by how much assistance we provide, but by how much independence communities gain.
